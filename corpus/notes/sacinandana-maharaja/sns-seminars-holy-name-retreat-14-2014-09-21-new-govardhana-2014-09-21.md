# SNS Seminars Holy Name Retreat 14 2014 09 21 New Govardhana Village

## Theme

Sacīnandana Mahārāja frames this Holy-Name retreat lecture around a single question: how does a transient spiritual experience become a lasting change in one's chanting? He opens by distinguishing two ways to attend a retreat or visit Vṛndāvana — merely passing through the exercises, or actively mining them for one inspiration to carry home. The foundation of that journey is faith: faith is the sole entrance gate into the holy name and bhakti, the bridge between the conditioned soul one is now and the blissful devotee one hopes to become — faith in the process, in Krishna, and in the very possibility of transformation. Invoking Einstein's definition of madness (repeating the same activity while expecting a different result), he warns that Krishna consciousness will not arrive automatically; sincere, changed effort is required, and the effort itself is an act of faith. He then diagnoses the obstacle: offenses are like clouds blocking the soul from the 'Krishna-sun,' and rather than treating all ten offenses he addresses their common root — inattention (anavadhāna) while chanting, which springs from indifference, restlessness of mind, and inertia. The remedies are the association of enthusiastic devotees and dedicated time alone with the holy name, approached scientifically through Harināma Cintāmaṇi and the bhūta-śuddhi discipline of withdrawing wrong attachment to body and mind. The lecture's central practical teaching addresses the inevitable return of the old self after an elevating experience: old habits and unsatisfactory chanting patterns will 'knock at the door,' and one needs a new habit already in place to meet them. Resolutions fail because motivation — the vast circle of everything we wish to change — always dwarfs willpower, the small area inside it; spread across many goals, willpower accomplishes nothing. Hence the watering-can image: remove the hundred-holed nozzle and the single concentrated stream becomes powerful. He therefore urges each devotee to leave with exactly ONE concrete, modest, written resolution within reach of their actual willpower — not 64 rounds, but the rounds one already chants made more attentive; even firm determination alone counts as a real beginning. He closes by widening the lens: real wealth is measured by consciousness, not money (the blissful widows and sadhus of Vṛndāvana); caring for the genuine needs of Vaiṣṇavas earns their blessings (the BrajCare program); meditation can be deliberately fed into japa by reading a relevant verse beforehand (the 'Spiritual Tonic' design); and sustainable practice rests on a balanced lifestyle that tends emotional, social, and physical needs so that chanting does not collapse into burnout.

## Practical application

Before leaving the retreat — and at the close of any intense spiritual experience — choose just ONE thing to bring into your daily japa, and pick something your willpower can genuinely sustain. Do not resolve on an ambitious quota like 64 rounds; instead resolve to chant the rounds you already chant with more attention, aiming at a 'lowest common denominator' you can actually keep every day. Make the resolution concrete and written: define specific, actionable steps, such as rising early and chanting before showering, setting up and beautifying a home altar, sitting on a cushion (or even a blanket), and chanting two truly concentrated rounds. Concentrate your effort like a single stream of water rather than dispersing it across many projects. To fight inattention (anavadhāna) at its root, cultivate the association of enthusiastic devotees and protect dedicated time alone with the holy name; treat chanting as a deliberate, systematic discipline — a 'scientist' of Harināma Cintāmaṇi — including bhūta-śuddhi, withdrawing identification with body and mind. Feed meditation directly into your rounds by reading a relevant verse just before and while chanting, so the mind is carried into vivid remembrance of Krishna, His associates, and Goloka Vṛndāvana rather than repeating mechanically. Expect the old self to return and have your new habit ready to answer the door. Finally, keep the practice sustainable by tending your emotional, social, and physical needs alongside chanting, so devotion deepens without burnout — and remember that even firmly fixing the resolve in your mind is already a real beginning.

## Glossary

- **Hari-Nāma** — The holy name of Krishna; the chanting of the names of God as the central practice of devotional life.
- **japa** — Soft, individual repetition of the holy name, typically counted on beads in daily rounds.
- **bhakti** — Loving devotional service to Krishna; the goal and path that faith opens the gate into.
- **anavadhāna** — Inattention or lack of attentiveness while chanting; identified as the common root of offenses, arising from indifference, mental restlessness, and inertia.
- **Harināma Cintāmaṇi** — Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's classic work on the holy name and its offenses; here invoked as the systematic 'science' of attentive chanting.
- **bhūta-śuddhi** — Purification of the elements; the meditative practice of withdrawing false attachment to and identification with the body and mind.
- **Vṛndāvana** — The sacred land of Krishna's pastimes in India; a holy place of pilgrimage and residence for devotees.
- **dhāma** — A holy abode of Krishna; residence in the dhāma is described as a source of spiritual wealth and blissful God consciousness.
- **bhajan** — Personal devotional worship and chanting; the engagement that makes the Vṛndāvana sadhus and widows spiritually rich.
- **Vaiṣṇava** — A devotee of Krishna (Vishnu); caring for their genuine needs is presented as a way to receive their blessings.
- **sadhu** — A holy person or renunciant devoted to spiritual life.
- **Goloka Vṛndāvana** — Krishna's supreme spiritual abode; the object of vivid remembrance when meditation is fed into japa.
- **Braj / BrajCare** — Braj is the greater Vṛndāvana region; BrajCare is the medical-card service program framed as a means to earn the blessings of Vṛndāvana's inhabitants.
