# SNS Holy Name Retreat Retreat 02 Tuning In 01 2018 11 27 GV

## Theme

The lecture opens the 'Tuning In' retreat by framing the retreat itself as a deliberate act of arrival — 'the time is now, the place is here, you have arrived.' Sacīnandana Mahārāja explains that ordinary life runs on fixed habits, desires, and expectations that form a small, self-constructed world, and that genuine spiritual progress requires consciously stepping out of that world to break old patterns (echoing Einstein's quip that repeating the same action while expecting a new result is madness). He describes the retreat as a 'liminal space' — an in-between, not-yet-enlightened state in which, precisely because the old life is left behind and the new is not yet attained, fresh paths and discoveries become possible. The central problem he diagnoses is that modern, outwardly-facing people are afraid to turn inward into the 'temple of the heart' where growth happens; the remedy is the fearless inward path of hearing spiritual subjects (śravaṇa), reflecting on and digesting them, and finally applying them, supported by minimizing external noise (phones, social media, news) to build a world made only of received spiritual inspiration. He then grounds the retreat's purpose in scripture: drawing on Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura's praise of an Eleventh-Canto verse, he teaches that bhakti — and above all chanting the holy name — is 'the supreme intelligence of the intelligent,' the unique process by which the temporary, perishable body and mind can be used in this very life to attain the eternal Lord. Though the body is 'not worth a penny' and will be ruined by age and disease, offering it to Krishna draws Krishna Himself — the ocean of sweetness — in return; thus 'giving the body to the Lord' means concretely engaging the senses in devotional service. Because the holy name is the foundation of all spiritual life and the only boat across the ocean of saṁsāra, the retreat is the work of building that foundation, which requires a clear resolve fixed from the very start. Mahārāja closes with the archery analogy — the mantra is the bow, the self the arrow, Krishna the target — unfolding chanting as three movements: take aim (clarify the goal of reaching Krishna), make effort (the discipline of practice, matched appropriately to oneself), and let go (surrender, allowing Krishna to give what He wishes). He warns that an expectant, demanding mindset that craves miraculous experiences is a mark of immature beginners and seals one off from the very lesson to be learned; the mature alternative is the humble 'receiver mood' of surrender.

## Practical application

For daily japa and Hari-Nāma, the devotee first 'arrives': sets aside the autopilot of habitual life and consciously creates a quieter outer environment by reducing phone calls, messaging, social media, and news so the inner space can open. Before chanting, follow the threefold archery movement. (1) Take aim — pause to ask 'Why am I here, and what do I want to reach in this foundational practice?', fixing a clear, specific resolve (e.g., 'this week I will apply myself sincerely to chanting and to improving my chanting') and holding to that single aim. (2) Make effort — match the type and intensity of effort to that aim and to your own present capacity, treating chanting as the patient, disciplined laying of a deep foundation rather than chasing quick results, and drawing on a chosen verse or motivation to hold steady when conditions turn unfavorable. (3) Let go — after aiming and exerting, relax and surrender, allowing Krishna to give exactly what He wishes instead of demanding a particular outcome. Concretely 'give the body to the Lord' by engaging the senses: the ears in hearing about Krishna and His name (śravaṇa), the mind in remembering and reflecting on what is heard — milling the concepts over, even into one's dreams — and the hands in His service. Finally, drop the craving for extraordinary or miraculous experiences during chanting; enter instead the 'receiver mood,' chanting humbly and attentively as one offering the temporary body and mind for the highest, eternal benefit.

## Glossary

- **Hari-Nāma / holy name** — The name of God (Hari/Krishna); chanting it is the foundational devotional practice and, per the lecture, the only boat to cross the ocean of material existence.
- **japa** — The practice of softly repeating or chanting the holy name, typically on beads — the foundational personal practice the retreat is built around.
- **bhakti** — Devotional service to the Lord; described here as 'the supreme intelligence of the intelligent,' the process that uses the temporary body and mind to attain the eternal Lord.
- **śravaṇa** — Hearing — receptively listening to spiritual subjects and the holy name; the first, fearless step of the inward turn, to be followed by reflection and application.
- **liminal space** — An in-between, threshold state in which one has left the old life but not yet realized the new; the uncertain condition of the retreat in which new paths and discoveries become possible.
- **saṁsāra** — The ocean of repeated material existence (birth, disease, old age, death); the holy name is the boat by which one crosses it.
- **Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura** — A revered Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava commentator, cited for his praise of an Eleventh-Canto verse and his commentary that the mortal body, though 'not worth a penny,' draws Krishna Himself when offered to Him.
- **Eleventh Canto** — The eleventh book of the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, source of the verse on using the human body and mind to attain the rare spiritual treasure of human life.
- **Kadamba Khaṇḍī** — A site in Vṛndāvana with a meditation cave, used as an illustration of the in-between (liminal) space where inner discovery happens.
- **Vṛndāvana** — The sacred land associated with Krishna's pastimes; here the geographic setting of the Kadamba Khaṇḍī meditation cave example.
