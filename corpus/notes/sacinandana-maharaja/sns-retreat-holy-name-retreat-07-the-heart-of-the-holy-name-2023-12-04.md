# SNS Retreat Holy Name Retreat 07 The Heart of the Holy Name 01 2023 12 04

## Theme

Titled 'The Heart of the Holy Name,' this opening session of HH Sacīnandana Mahārāja's Holy Name Retreat frames the holy name as an 'open secret' — a door standing three-times closed even though the name is fully available to all. Mahārāja diagnoses why the name stays shut: we are distracted and chant past it, we do not really know how to chant, or, most often, we are not yet permitted to taste its sweetness, so our chanting never rises above dutiful repetition. The remedy he offers begins with intention (chant to enter a relationship with Krishna, not merely because everyone does), with the body (a prāṇāyāma exercise drawn from Sanātana Gosvāmī, sitting straight and using the breath), and with place (cultivating awareness that this land, sprinkled with the dust of Krishna's lotus feet, his tears of longing for Rādhārāṇī, and the tears of centuries of yearning devotees, grants swift advancement). The doctrinal spine of the retreat is Śrīla Prabhupāda's Upadeśāmṛta and its verse on chanting. Following Rūpa Gosvāmī, Mahārāja develops the jaundice analogy: Krishna's names, form, and qualities are transcendentally sweet like sugar candy, but a tongue diseased by avidyā (ignorance) cannot relish them — just as jaundice produces bhojana-aruci (distaste for food, most acute toward sweets), avidyā produces sādhana-aruci, a distaste felt most sharply toward the very practice where Krishna is most present, the holy name. The cure is not to abandon the medicine but to keep taking it under two conditions Rūpa Gosvāmī names: anudina (chanting day after day) and ādarāt (with affectionate respect) — of which, Mahārāja contends, the movement grasps only 'one and a half,' having mastered regularity but largely missed ādara. He anatomizes avidyā through its five eternal functions from the Bhāgavatam (tama, moha, mahā-moha, tāmisra, andha-tāmisra), urging soul-searching self-inquiry rather than theory. He distinguishes merely avoiding the ten offenses ('negative life is not positive life,' neutral gear) from the positive ingredient of chanting carefully, with affectionate respect — instruction Prabhupāda reserves not for the casually interested but for the initiated who have entered bhajana-kriyā after dīkṣā. The decisive variable is sincerity: progress depends not on elapsed time but on taking prayojana-tattva, the goal of Krishna consciousness, as the soul's highest necessity. When the goal is felt as genuine necessity, effort and taste follow naturally, and the true price of Kṛṣṇa-prema is intense longing — manifesting visibly as tears of eagerness. Mahārāja distinguishes three tears (of the body, of the mind, and of the awakened soul), only the last being sweet and washing away the coverings of ignorance. For those in whom tears do not yet come, he turns to six practices for developing japa (from the Mantra-nava cited in Hari-bhakti-vilāsa, with Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's commentary), beginning with the first and foundational one, mana-saṃharaṇam — withdrawing the mind from matter. He grounds this in the insight that we never perceive reality directly but only our mind's interpretation of it ('the world is not how you see it, the world is how you are'), counsels not taking the mind's praise-and-criticism verdicts too seriously, and warns that fault-finding and jealousy poison the heart (illustrated by the Amogha episode with Sarvabhauma Bhaṭṭācārya and Mahāprabhu). Knowing one 'should control the mind' is not the same as knowing how; so he offers a concrete three-step method — acknowledge the specific agitation, step back and view it from the level of eternal truth ('I am an eternal soul; this is a passing cloud'), then fill the freed space with the holy name, Kṛṣṇa-kathā, or service — and points to an authorized breathing exercise (deferred to the next day). He directs the meditation inward to the heart, where both the soul and Paramātmā reside, asking 'Who are you when everything that is not you falls away?', and roots confidence in chanting in whom one belongs to: the merciful Divine Couple — Krishna the ocean of mercy and Rādhā so compassionate she tends an injured calf — whose names japa directly addresses. He closes by re-prescribing the two practices for the tasteless (chant regularly despite the bitterness; cultivate affectionate respect, culminating in tears), by counseling not to wait until old age ('what you practice now is what you become'), by showing how manaḥ-saṁharaṇam serves even praying for another (an ailing devotee in the ICU), reframing the passing of senior godbrothers (Bhūrijana Prabhu's 'decade of tears') as transition rather than loss, and by urging that practical advice be acted on immediately, for advice practiced in the moment is what 'sticks' and becomes lasting habit.

## Practical application

Approach each session of japa or Hari-Nāma not as a duty to discharge but as an attempt to enter a relationship with Krishna — so examine your intention before you begin. Prepare the body and the setting: sit straight, take a few full, deep breaths to settle awareness, and cultivate the consciousness that you are chanting on sacred ground in the company of centuries of longing devotees. Then chant with full attention and heartfelt sincerity — 'like you really mean it' — rather than mechanically. Accept Rūpa Gosvāmī's two conditions as your daily standard: anudina, never breaking the regularity (chant every day, day after day), and ādarāt, the affectionate respect most of us neglect — chant remembering Krishna's name with care and gratitude in a prayerful posture. Do not let a vague sense of 'some offense I must be committing' breed despair: unintentional lapses count less, Krishna is not a nitpicking god, and the prescribed shelter for accidental nāmāparādha is simply constant, uninterrupted chanting, which itself destroys the offenses. But do not stop at merely avoiding the ten offenses — that is only neutral gear; add the positive ingredient of careful, respectful, affectionate chanting. Keep the goal (prayojana-tattva — Krishna consciousness) consciously before you throughout the day as the soul's real necessity, subordinating bodily and mental needs to it; when the goal is felt as a true necessity, taste and effort follow on their own, and the aim becomes not just to chant but to chant with relish and then share that taste with others. Where taste and tears have not yet come, take the medicine anyway and work on the first of the six japa practices: manaḥ-saṃharaṇam — actively withdraw the wandering mind from material things and from your social roles, status, anxieties, the pull of social-media approval, even strong infatuations, and re-fix it on the sound of the names; emptying is only preparation for filling that cleared space with the holy name. When a specific disturbance grips the mind during chanting, use the three steps in the moment: (1) acknowledge and name the exact agitation without suppressing it, (2) step back and view it from the level of truth ('this is temporary; I am an eternal soul; this upset is a passing cloud'), (3) immediately fill the freed space with the holy name, Kṛṣṇa-kathā, or service so it cannot return — acknowledge, step back, fill with Krishna. Guard the heart as a clean place, refusing to admit negativity, fault-finding, or jealousy in the first place, since that makes withdrawing the mind far easier. Draw the attention inward to the heart, where the soul and Paramātmā reside, and chant as a direct, prayerful address to Rādhā and Krishna, to whom you belong. Do not postpone this practice to old age — what you practice now is what you become — and whenever you receive a piece of practical spiritual advice (including the simple instruction to withdraw the mind, or to finish rounds you have left undone), act on it at once, because advice practiced in the moment is what turns instruction into lasting habit.

## Glossary

- **Upadeśāmṛta (The Nectar of Instruction)** — Śrīla Prabhupāda's edition of Rūpa Gosvāmī's eleven-verse work; the text at the heart of the retreat, whose verse on chanting is the foundation of the presentation.
- **avidyā** — Ignorance; the covering of the material energy over the soul that diseases the 'tongue' so it cannot taste the sweetness of the holy name, producing distaste for spiritual practice.
- **japa** — Soft, personal recitation of the holy name on beads; the daily chanting practice the six recommended methods are meant to develop.
- **kīrtana** — Congregational or sung chanting of the holy name.
- **Hare Krishna mahā-mantra** — The 'great chant for deliverance'; the mantra the initiated devotee regularly chants as the substance of bhajana-kriyā.
- **prāṇāyāma** — Authorized breathing exercise, recommended by Sanātana Gosvāmī in Hari-bhakti-vilāsa and noted by Prabhupāda (Third Canto, Bhāgavatam) as purifying the mind; used here to settle and withdraw the agitated mind before chanting.
- **ādara / ādarāt** — Affectionate respect; one of Rūpa Gosvāmī's two conditions for the name to act as medicine (Prabhupāda renders it 'carefully'; Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura as 'affectionate respect') — the positive ingredient Mahārāja says the movement largely misses.
- **anudina** — Daily, day after day; Rūpa Gosvāmī's other condition — chanting with unbroken regularity.
- **anādara** — The absence of care or respect; one of the ten offenses against the holy name.
- **aparādha / nāmāparādha** — Offense / offense against the holy name; for unintentional nāmāparādha the prescribed remedy (Padma Purāṇa, via Hari-bhakti-vilāsa) is sole shelter in constant chanting, which destroys the offenses.
- **bhojana-aruci** — Distaste for food, most pronounced toward sweets, produced by jaundice; the bodily analogue of sādhana-aruci.
- **sādhana-aruci** — Distaste for spiritual practice produced by avidyā, felt most acutely toward chanting the holy name, where Krishna is most fully present.
- **tama** — First function of avidyā: ignorance of the true self — accepting in theory that one is not the body yet unable to positively know one's identity, living in false ego.
- **moha** — Second function of avidyā: mistaking the body for the self.
- **mahā-moha** — Third function of avidyā: the desire for material enjoyment (eating, sleeping, defending, mating) arising from bodily identification.
- **tāmisra** — Fourth function of avidyā: the anger that arises when the attempt to enjoy is frustrated.
- **andha-tāmisra** — Fifth function of avidyā: feeling oneself dead and purposeless when enjoyments are forcibly snatched away.
- **dīkṣā** — Spiritual initiation on the path of bhakti; the positive instruction on careful, respectful chanting is meant for the seriously initiated.
- **bhajana-kriyā** — The stage after dīkṣā in which the sincerely initiated devotee takes up the Lord's service by regularly chanting the mahā-mantra and following all regulations.
- **prayojana-tattva** — The ultimate goal — Krishna consciousness — taken as the soul's highest necessity; sincerity means keeping this aim before oneself throughout the day.
- **Kṛṣṇa-prema** — Pure love of Krishna, whose true 'price' is not pious activity but intense, sincere longing — visibly manifest as tears of eagerness.
- **mana-saṃharaṇam / manaḥ-saṁharaṇa** — Withdrawing or concentrating the mind, taking it from matter and re-fixing it on the sound of the names; the first of the six japa practices.
- **sampatti** — Attainment / spiritual advancement in japa that the six recommended qualities are said to yield.
- **Hari-bhakti-vilāsa** — Devotional manual (by Sanātana Gosvāmī) cited as the source of the breathing exercise and, at 17.129 with Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's commentary, of the verse from the Mantra-nava listing the six japa practices.
- **Paramātmā** — The Supersoul; the Lord's localized aspect who, along with the individual soul, resides in the heart space where the inward meditation is directed.
- **sannyāsa** — The renounced order; the confidential teaching of Upadeśāmṛta was first restricted to sannyāsa disciples before Prabhupāda ordered 100,000 copies for mass distribution.
- **Śrīmatī Rādhārāṇī (Rādhā)** — The Queen of Vṛndāvana and supremely compassionate consort of Krishna; the Divine Couple addressed directly in chanting, in whose longing Krishna sheds the tears that sprinkle the sacred dust.
- **kumkum** — Sacred red powder; in the illustration of Rādhā's compassion, the freshly ground kumkum with which she dresses an injured calf's wound.
- **nu** — The Sanskrit particle in the verse that Mahārāja renders 'oh, how wonderful it is' (linked to the German 'nanu'), keying his framing of wonder and astonishment toward the holy name.
