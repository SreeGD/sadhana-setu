# SNS Retreat Holy Name Retreat 01 Entering the Retreat 2023 08 16 Slovenia

## Theme

Mahārāja opens the Holy Name Retreat by distinguishing it sharply from an ordinary festival, feast, lecture, or seminar: a retreat (anuṣṭhāna) means giving all of one's focus to deepen a single devotional practice, and even its remote, distraction-free setting is part of the discipline, forcing a real decision to commit. He invokes the tradition's most rigorous chanting retreats — three months, 3 a.m. to midnight, eating once a day, the vow restarted if even broken by speech — as a mirror for our 'comfort society, lazy society for spiritual life.' The retreat's true aim, he insists, is not improvement in the abstract but to meet Kṛṣṇa face to face through His holy name, which becomes possible when one learns to chant free of offenses. The two qualities that make chanting effective are attentiveness and sincerity, citing Śrīla Prabhupāda that the more attentively and sincerely one chants, the more spiritual progress one makes. Everyone genuinely wants to chant sincerely and be blissful, but, not knowing how, seeks that bliss everywhere except in the name itself. Chanting is presented as a heartfelt prayer to Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa to be freed from Māyā and engaged in Their service; as the very essence of Kṛṣṇa consciousness (always remember, never forget); and as an art — sound has real potency that must be skillfully learned, for proper chanting purifies the heart into a place where God is directly manifested, while unskilled or offensive chanting can backfire into greater material attachment. Among all paths — jñāna, karma, yoga, aṣṭāṅga-yoga — the holy name alone yields love of God and is the only effective process in Kali-yuga; ninety percent of one's advancement depends on it, and the order to chant sixteen rounds daily is the most essential of the spiritual master's instructions. Mahārāja then diagnoses why this 'easiest' process feels hard: mental blocks, especially the false security of assuming initiation grants an automatic ticket back to Godhead. He surveys four blocking attitudes (change is not needed / not possible / too hard / will happen automatically) and five types of chanter — inattentive, lazy, indifferent, unaware, and the rare committed chanter — as a tool for honest self-examination, warning that an uncommitted mentality leaves one a permanent frustrated spectator at the window, never tasting the nectar. The remedy is the retreat's central I-S-A formula: Intention (sankalpa — resolving beforehand 'this is my time with Kṛṣṇa'), Sincerity (no pretense, humbly calling out as a fallen soul), and Attention (sustained as much as one can). By the law of reciprocation, Kṛṣṇa responds the instant He sees these three and asks no more — where your focus goes, He Himself supplies the energy, so one never chants alone. Mahārāja sets a reflective exercise contrasting the benefit of changing one's japa habits against the cost of leaving them unchanged, urging participants to truly 'work the mind.' He frames chanting as a living relationship to be felt, not a performance to be watched, affirmed by a participant's testimony of a 'day and night' transformation through surrender to the Name. He closes with practical counsel for the retreat days: minimize distractions, sleep enough and eat light, make personal space in solitude and silence, allow inner room for realizations, cultivate positive rather than fault-finding thoughts, use all time for spiritual purpose, and make a concrete personal saṅkalpa to improve one's relationship with the Holy Name. Ultimately the retreat is one's own — the teacher is only a servant — yet simply being present is already Kṛṣṇa's mercy: once one has 'booked the seat on the train,' the process itself will carry the practitioner to the destination.

## Practical application

Approach each japa session as a retreat in miniature: before beginning, consciously set your intention — 'this is my time with Kṛṣṇa' — with an unmixed motive simply to connect, unlike kīrtana where the desire for praise can creep in. Chant sincerely, dropping all pretense: honestly admit you are trying but not yet successful, and humbly call out as a fallen soul, 'My dear Lord, please help this soul, please accept me.' Then sustain as much attention as you honestly can, treating the Hare Krishna mahā-mantra as a heartfelt prayer to Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa to be disengaged from Māyā and engaged in Their service, and relate to the Name as a person you are in living relationship with rather than a quota to finish. Trust the law of reciprocation — the moment Kṛṣṇa sees your intention, sincerity, and attention, He supplies the energy Himself, so you are never chanting alone. Use the five types of chanter as an honest mirror ('where am I?') and refuse the four mental excuses (that change is unneeded, impossible, too hard, or automatic); like a pūjārī who will not eat before completing his worship, make your daily sixteen rounds a non-negotiable priority and resolve that they will be genuinely good rounds. Protect the quality of the mind: minimize contact with mundane things (worldly books, films, sense gratification) whose energy the mind absorbs, sleep enough and eat light so a tired body does not make a tired mind, and deliberately make space — solitude, silence, time by flowing water, journaling, prayer — so deep realizations and a deepening relationship with the Name have room to arise. Cultivate compassion, forgiveness, and gratitude toward fellow devotees rather than a critical, fault-finding mind. Periodically weigh the benefit of changing your chanting habits against the cost of leaving them unchanged, and make one concrete personal saṅkalpa to improve your relationship with the Holy Name. Remember that hearing lectures and knowing intellectually that Kṛṣṇa exists is not the same as experiencing Him — the gap is closed only by going deeper into the chanting yourself; and even an imperfect chanter with a restless, critical mind finds that everything changes when attention is focused on the name, because Kṛṣṇa loves and delights in our chanting regardless of our perceived qualification.

## Glossary

- **anuṣṭhāna** — A retreat or sustained disciplined observance — giving all of one's focus and concentration to deepen a single devotional practice, as distinct from a festival, feast, lecture, or seminar.
- **japa** — Personal, often soft or private chanting of the holy name on beads; here demanding an unmixed intention simply to connect with Kṛṣṇa.
- **kīrtana** — Congregational or musical chanting of the holy name; valued in the retreat but, unlike japa, prone to mixed motives such as hoping to be praised.
- **Hare Krishna mantra / Mahā-mantra** — The great chant ('Hare Kṛṣṇa, Hare Kṛṣṇa…'); a heartfelt prayer to Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa to be freed from Māyā and engaged in Their service, and the means of constant remembrance of Kṛṣṇa.
- **Kṛṣṇa** — The Supreme Lord (God) whom the retreat aims to meet face to face through His holy name.
- **Rādhā** — Kṛṣṇa's eternal consort; the mahā-mantra is addressed to Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa together.
- **Māyā** — Material illusion or entanglement; chanting is a prayer to be disengaged from Māyā and engaged in the Lord's service.
- **sankalpa / saṅkalpa** — A conscious resolve or intention — both the 'this is my time with Kṛṣṇa' resolve before chanting (the 'I' of the I-S-A formula) and the concrete personal vow made for the retreat to improve one's relationship with the Holy Name.
- **Kali-yuga** — The present degraded age, in which chanting the holy name is taught to be the only effective process of self-realization.
- **jñāna** — The path of philosophical knowledge; listed among processes of self-realization said to be insufficient in Kali-yuga compared to chanting.
- **karma** — The path of (fruitive) action; listed among the self-realization processes surpassed by chanting the holy name.
- **yoga / aṣṭāṅga-yoga** — The yoga path, including the eightfold (aṣṭāṅga) system; counted among the methods of self-realization that, unlike chanting, do not yield love of God in this age.
- **Gāyatrī** — A potent Vedic mantra (for the twice-initiated) cited as an example that properly chanted sound has real power, even to heal disease.
- **pūjārī** — A temple priest/worshiper of the deities; invoked as the model of the committed chanter who will not proceed with life (or eat) until the worship is complete, and who offers the lamp during ārati.
- **ārati** — The ceremony of offering lamp and articles to the deities; those without a physical lamp are told to mentally offer the light along with the pūjārī so all participate inwardly.
- **Jīva Gosvāmī** — A foundational teacher (ācārya) of the tradition, cited for the teaching that chanting the holy name is uniquely the chief means of attaining love of God.
- **Śrīla Prabhupāda** — The founding spiritual master quoted throughout — that attentive, sincere chanting brings spiritual progress, that sincere chanting can let one meet Kṛṣṇa, and that the order to chant sixteen rounds daily is the most essential instruction.
