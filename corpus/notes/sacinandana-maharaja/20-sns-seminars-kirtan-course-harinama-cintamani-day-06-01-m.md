# 20 SNS Seminars Kirtan Course Harinama Cintamani Day 06 01 Morning Lecture ISKCON Radhadesh

## Theme

This lecture develops a single, central conviction of Kṛṣṇa consciousness: 'I am an eternal soul, not this body.' Mahārāja begins by locating the root of material bondage in ahaṁ-mama — the false sense of 'I and mine' — showing that this proprietary illusion is what binds the soul to matter in every position, high or low. He stresses that even liberated souls are not automatically immune, citing Dhruva Mahārāja, who fell temporarily under māyā and identified by bodily relations; the forgetful soul is like a man with amnesia who adopts a false identity. The remedy, however, is not a hard-fought battle but simple devotional service to the Lord, the Supersoul dwelling in all beings, which dissolves 'I and mine' and restores the true relationship: 'I am His servant, He is my master.' To approach the supremely pure Kṛṣṇa, one must oneself become spiritualized — 'devam bhūtvā devaṁ yajet,' to enter fire one must become fire — which means shedding all material designations of nationality, mood, and body (echoed in Meister Eckhart's teaching that one must be 'dead' to be with God). Mahārāja grounds this in scripture (Hari-bhakti-vilāsa, Sanātana Gosvāmī): without purification of identity, japa, homa, and other practices bear no fruit (niṣphala). He then presents the tradition's remedy, bhūta-śuddhi — the dismantling of the false identity and reconstruction of the true one — contrasting the fiery tāntric version (used only as illustration) with the gentle Vaiṣṇava meditation, in which one recognizes oneself as Kṛṣṇa's eternal servant, fallen since time immemorial and now restored through the spiritual master's mercy. He warns that neglecting this realization keeps devotees as perpetual beginners identifying with sickness and moods (Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī's boat-with-anchor analogy; Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's three meditations), and that the danger of 'I and mine' deepens the further one is absorbed. Crucially, the realization must move from theory to direct experience, attained not by willpower or bodily strength but through helpless prayer and dependence — as when his own exhausted prayer to Rādhā Madana-mohana dissolved hunger and thirst, and his finest round of a 64-rounds day came only after intellectual understanding was joined to heartfelt surrender. Fixed in the eternal self, one is untroubled by illness, moods, or harsh words, and even unshaken at death — embodied in Śrīla Prabhupāda, who laughed with love rather than fear in his final days and whose last instruction to the sannyasīs was that one must first understand one is not the body before any further talk of philosophy. This is buddhi-śuddhi, the purification of intelligence, from which devotional practice proceeds and illusion slips away.

## Practical application

The devotee's daily japa and Hari-Nāma are meant to be the very ground on which the conviction 'I am not this body, I am Kṛṣṇa's eternal servant' is realized — not merely recited. Before or during chanting, practice the Vaiṣṇava bhūta-śuddhi meditation: recognize that by nature you are the eternal servant of Kṛṣṇa, that by misfortune you turned away since time immemorial and wandered in birth and death, and that through the spiritual master's mercy you now know yourself as completely separate from the gross and subtle body, serving Caitanya Mahāprabhu and your worshipable deities. Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's three meditations offer a simple daily formula: (1) I am part and parcel of Kṛṣṇa, a spark of His fire; (2) Kṛṣṇa is my most merciful Lord and master; (3) I am in this world for one purpose — to be purified — which is why I chant. When sickness, bad moods, or others' harsh words arise, refuse to let them stop the practice: label them plainly as 'only the body' or 'only the disturbed mind,' and apply spiritual practice precisely there rather than pleading 'I'm sick, I can't come.' Because realization cannot be forced by willpower, convert intellectual understanding into experience through heartfelt, helpless prayer — asking Kṛṣṇa to let you truly realize this and to accept you despite your disqualification. Sustain the process with steady discipline (chanting in the morning, reading in the evening), which reignites conviction, and use voluntary austerities such as nirjala ekādaśī with increased rounds to feel experientially that you are not the body. Guard against seemingly innocent bodily cravings and material absorption in 'I and mine,' which only deepen the danger. Rightly identified as the servant of the servant of the servant of Kṛṣṇa, the anchor of false identification is lifted and the boat of devotional practice finally moves — the japa bears fruit instead of remaining niṣphala.

## Glossary

- **ahaṁ-mama** — The conception of 'I and mine' — the false sense of self and proprietorship over the body and its extensions that is the root of attraction to matter and bondage to the material world.
- **māyā** — Illusion; the deluding energy that makes the soul forget its real identity and mistake itself for the body, gripping even those who have advanced spiritually.
- **Supersoul (Paramātmā)** — The Lord as the reservoir of all pleasure dwelling within all beings; by simple devotional service to Him the false conception of 'I and mine' is eradicated.
- **devam bhūtvā devaṁ yajet** — A verse from the Skanda Purāṇa meaning that to worship a divine being one must oneself become spiritualized — giving up wrong material conceptions; illustrated by Prabhupāda as 'to enter the fire you must become fire.'
- **rāsa dance** — Kṛṣṇa's dance of loving exchange; its happiness can be shared only by one who has first become purified, since only a pure devotee can approach the supremely pure Lord.
- **Meister Eckhart** — A German Christian mystic cited for the teaching that one who would be with God must be 'dead' — not physically, but through the dying away of the illusory concepts 'I am the body' and 'this is mine.'
- **Hari-bhakti-vilāsa** — A Vaiṣṇava scripture on devotional practice, cited to establish that without purification of identity, japa, homa, and other practices bear no fruit.
- **niṣphala** — Fruitless; describing spiritual practices (japa, sacrifices) that yield no result when performed without purification of one's identity, because the chains of illusion remain uncut.
- **bhūta-śuddhi** — Purification of the material elements — the dismantling of the false identity ('I am the body / my mind') and the reconstruction of the true spiritual identity; a practice found across Indian traditions in differing forms.
- **buddhi-śuddhi** — Purification of intelligence — the breaking of attachment to 'I and me,' after which devotional practice causes illusions to slip away and one acts from the platform of the eternal self.
- **amṛta** — Nectar; in the tāntric bhūta-śuddhi illustration, the drop that descends from the Lord onto the ashes of the burnt material body, from which the true spiritual identity emerges.
- **japa** — The practice of chanting the Holy Name on beads; central to Hari-Nāma sādhana, but fruitful only when joined to realization of one's true spiritual identity.
- **homa** — Vedic fire sacrifice; cited alongside japa as a pious practice that bears no fruit without prior purification of identity.
- **sattva / mode of goodness** — The mode of goodness; distinguishes the sāttvic tāntric bhūta-śuddhi Mahārāja describes for illustration from the tamasic (mode of ignorance) 'tantra' popularly known in the West.
- **nirjala ekādaśī** — An ekādaśī observed without food or water; a voluntary austerity, often paired with increased rounds of chanting, undertaken to experientially realize that one is not the body.
- **sannyāsī** — A member of the renounced order of life; the audience to whom Prabhupāda gave his last instruction that one must first understand one is not the body.
- **Rādhā Madana-mohana** — The presiding deities to whom Mahārāja prayed in exhaustion and shame, whereupon hunger and thirst vanished and he directly realized himself as Kṛṣṇa's eternal servant.
- **servant of the servant of the servant of Kṛṣṇa (dāsānudāsa)** — The correct self-identification of the soul — a blissful part of the Lord and the servant of His servants — as opposed to the 'pile of ashes' of the adorned material body.
