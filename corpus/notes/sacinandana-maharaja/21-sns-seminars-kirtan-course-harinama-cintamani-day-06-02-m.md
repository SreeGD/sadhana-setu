# 21 SNS Seminars Kirtan Course Harinama Cintamani Day 06 02 Morning Session ISKCON Radhadesh

## Theme

This morning session, drawn from the study of Harināma Cintāmaṇi (Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura), moves from diagnosis to remedy to lived practice. It opens with the tenth offense to the holy name — attachment to 'I and mine,' the identification with the body and its extensions — which leaves a person unable to develop attraction to the name even after hearing its glory. Mahārāja frames the danger with the analogy of climbing the wrong mountain: sincere effort along a false conception can carry one high and yet nowhere near the true goal, so the offense is removed only by climbing the right mountain — complete surrender to the holy name. He then unfolds śaraṇāgati through its six limbs: accepting all that is favorable to the Lord's service (including material arrangements like marriage that genuinely help one advance, rather than forced, unsuited renunciation), rejecting with disgust all that is unfavorable, and above all depending on Krishna as sole maintainer and protector — the point from which, per Jīva Gosvāmī, all other limbs become easy — culminating in humility, knowing oneself as the tiny soul, one ten-thousandth the tip of a hair. That minuteness, he clarifies, is a seed containing a great tree: one's full eternal identity lies latent within and is progressively revealed through practice, the first sign being a distinct attraction to a particular form or pastime of Krishna. The way up is always the way down — coming closer to the humble position of a tiny part of Krishna, chanting without material motivation until 'I' and 'mine' dissolve and one crosses Māyā. Throughout, Mahārāja insists the holy name is not a technique to make material life more peaceful but a means to wake from the dream of material existence; one must first become a proper human being, confronting rather than fleeing one's humanness, so that realization becomes lived reality and not a temporary construction over an unaddressed nature. Realizations, once received, must be digested — asking what happened, how it felt, and how it changed one's life. The lecture then turns from theory to practice: the story of the king's search for Krishna's 'best temple,' which proves to be the heart of a simple chanting Vaiṣṇava who builds and worships the Lord within, and whose pleading, longing chant culminates in the Lord appearing on the altar of his heart. From this, Mahārāja leads a guided meditation — 'construct a temple in the heart' — using the 'three baths of the soul' given by Nārada Muni (developed for the Varṣāṇā and Govardhan retreats): purification of the heart, not austerity alone, admits one to Rādhā-Krishna's reality, as Nārada himself learned at Kusuma-sarovara. Bathing on Vṛndā-devī's instruction, he shed his material identity and emerged an eternal servant fit to behold the Divine Couple. The practice proceeds through breath work to become the neutral observer, a visualized immersion in the Yamunā — a spiritual river of love that vanquishes offenses — through three dips dissolving bodily/social, mental, and egoic identity, the bhūta-śuddhi contemplation of oneself as Krishna's eternal servant distinct from gross and subtle body, darśana of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa beneath the desire tree, and kīrtana offered in the mood of ātma-nivedana. It closes on integration: connected kīrtana carried beyond the session into all of life, conscious absorption of the mood afterward, reflective sharing with a partner, and Prabhupāda's teaching that one cannot renounce the body while embodied but can act on the spiritual platform through a shift in consciousness — connecting every activity to Krishna so that all twenty-four hours become meditation, work itself becoming remembrance. Cooking offered to the Deity first, done for Krishna's pleasure, is the same mystery as kīrtana: the activity is unchanged, only the consciousness behind it is switched, and at that moment everything changes.

## Practical application

Begin by examining the root offense the lecture targets: the sense of 'I and mine,' the reflexive identification with the body, its relationships, nationality, and possessions. Rather than fighting this abstractly, apply śaraṇāgati concretely — accept in your life whatever genuinely helps your service (do not force an unsuited renunciation that could destabilize you), and reject with real aversion the associations and enjoyments that pull you back into material absorption. Take shelter of Krishna as your actual maintainer and protector; from that dependence the rest of surrender grows easier. Cultivate the humble self-understanding of being a tiny part of Krishna, and let your eternal identity clarify naturally through practice rather than manufacturing details. For japa and Hari-Nāma itself, treat the name not as a relaxation technique but as a longing, pleading appeal for the Lord's personal mercy — chant as the Vaiṣṇava in the story chants: 'I have called Your name so long; will You now show Your mercy?' Structure your sitting: first settle the body with breath work, lengthening the exhale and simply observing the air, becoming a neutral observer so the mind's attachments recede — knowing this is mechanical preparation, not the meditation itself. Then perform bhūta-śuddhi: contemplate that you are by nature the eternal servant of Krishna who, through misfortune, has identified with the body and wandered in birth and death, but by the spiritual master's mercy now know yourself as distinct from gross and subtle body. Use the Yamunā immersion as a felt purification — visualize entering the sacred river of love, dipping three times to wash away bodily/social identity, then mental concepts and plans, then ego — praying to her as an active agent of purification, not scenery. Establish darśana of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa beneath the desire tree, offer everything to Them in the mood of ātma-nivedana ('whatever is mine is Yours; nothing is for my own enjoyment'), and let the chanting become worship rather than performance. Aim for 'connected,' absorbed chanting and notice the rare shift from thinking to singing; when critical, distracting thoughts arise (as they do even for leaders), tolerate rather than react — it is through tolerance that the mind is freed to leave the world. Afterward, do not rush off: breathe and consciously absorb the mood the chanting created, then reflect — what happened, how it felt, how it changed you — and, where possible, share honestly with another devotee, since we learn what helps us connect from one another. Finally, extend the practice beyond the mat: you cannot abandon the body, so shift consciousness instead — connect every daily activity to Krishna, cook for Him and offer to the Deity before tasting, do the unavoidable duties first for Him, so that all twenty-four hours, work included, become remembrance and constant Hari-Nāma.

## Glossary

- **Harināma Cintāmaṇi** — A scripture by Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura on the science and offenses of chanting the holy name; the primary source text studied in this lecture (esp. ch. 13, on the tenth offense).
- **Bhajana-rahasya** — A devotional work by Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura, cited alongside Harināma Cintāmaṇi, on the confidential process of worship and progressive stages of bhajana.
- **Nāma-aparādha (offense to the holy name)** — An offense committed against the chanting of the holy name; the tenth listed here is attachment to 'I and mine,' bodily identification that blocks attraction to the name.
- **Śaraṇāgati** — Surrender to the Lord, structured in six limbs — accepting the favorable, rejecting the unfavorable, faith in Krishna as protector and as maintainer, full self-surrender, and humility.
- **Ātma-nivedana** — Complete surrender of the self — offering one's very being to the Lord; the mood in which one tells Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa that everything one has is Their property, for Their enjoyment alone.
- **Bhūta-śuddhi** — Purification of self-conception — the meditative contemplation that one is the eternal servant of Krishna, distinct from the gross and subtle body, restoring one's capacity for service.
- **Kīrtana** — Congregational or personal chanting/singing of the holy names; meant to be conscious and 'connected' as an offering to please Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa, not a mechanical or musical performance.
- **Darśana** — Sacred seeing — here, the inner vision cultivated before chanting of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa seated on an effulgent throne beneath the desire tree with Their confidential associates.
- **Māyā** — The Lord's illusory material energy; the dream of material existence and bodily identification from which chanting the holy name is meant to awaken the soul.
- **Yamunā** — The sacred river of Vṛndāvana, daughter of the sun-god; understood to exist also in the spiritual world as a 'river of love' that bestows love of Krishna and vanquishes offenses and sinful reactions.
- **Vṛndāvana** — The transcendental abode (Dhāma) of Krishna's pastimes; the setting of the guided meditation on the bank of the Yamunā.
- **Vṛndā-devī** — The presiding goddess of Vṛndāvana who, in the narrated pastime, instructs Nārada Muni to bathe and shed his material identity before he can behold Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa.
- **Nārada Muni** — The celebrated sage-devotee whose 'three baths of the soul' at Kusuma-sarovara form the basis of the meditation; despite great austerities he could not see the Divine Couple until his heart was purified.
- **Kusuma-sarovara** — A sacred lake in the Vraja region where, in the narrated pastime, Nārada Muni bathed and gave up his material self-identification.
- **Rādhā-Śyāmasundara / Rādhā-Krishna** — The Divine Couple — Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa (Śyāmasundara, the beautiful dark-hued Lord); the supreme object of the devotee's meditation and service, in the footsteps of Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu.
- **Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu** — The founder of the saṅkīrtana (holy-name) movement, whose mood of servitude one follows in serving Rādhā-Śyāmasundara.
- **Jīva Gosvāmī** — A foremost Vaiṣṇava theologian who teaches that from the limb of accepting Krishna as protector and maintainer, all the other limbs of surrender become easy to practice.
- **Brahmacārī** — A celibate student/monk in the renounced-in-training āśrama; cited as an example where forcing renunciation on one unsuited to it can drive a person away from Krishna consciousness.
- **Vaiṣṇava** — A devotee of Viṣṇu/Krishna; exemplified by the simple chanter whose heart, made into a temple, the Lord prefers over any grand temple of stone.
- **Prabhupāda** — Śrīla A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda, cited for the teaching that one acts on the spiritual platform not by abandoning the body but by connecting every activity — cooking, eating, working — to Krishna.
- **Pūjā** — Ritual worship; referenced through the morning offering of incense whose smoke vanishes quickly — an image for the impermanence of all worldly relationships.
- **Deity** — The worshipable form of the Lord; food is offered to the Deity first before being honored, illustrating the switch of consciousness that spiritualizes ordinary activity.
