# SNS Seminars Harinaam Cintamani 03 2016 07 06

## Theme

This third session on Haridāsa Ṭhākura's Harinām Cintāmaṇi treats a single obstacle in chanting the holy name — inattention (pramāda): distractedness, restlessness, and indifference ('I just don't care any longer'). Following Haridāsa Ṭhākura, the speaker frames this inattention as pramāda, glossed through 'mada' (madness): the conditioned soul's original turning away from Krishna, likened to a fish jumping out of water. This turning-away is the root disease and the very cause of material existence, and we repeat it even toward the holy name — Krishna's most merciful form — by letting attention drift as we chant. Inattention is therefore not a minor lapse but the continuation of that original rejection; it is the root of all other chanting offenses (even one free of every other offense may not attain love of God while pramāda remains), and it pains Krishna because it re-enacts the first rejection. Curing it is not a mechanical or yogic technique but the healing of an attitude of the heart. Haridāsa Ṭhākura distinguishes two kinds of souls — those who imagine independent happiness apart from Krishna (Maya's side, however disguised) and those who seek pleasure only in His association — and each devotee must honestly assess which he is. A litmus test learned from Bhurijana Prabhu reveals this: when in difficulty, where do you actually take shelter? Krishna taught exclusive shelter in Him through the Govardhan-līlā, stopping Nanda Mahārāja's Indra-prayers ('take shelter in Me and I will protect you') and holding the hill for seven days to correspond to seven false shelters (anya-āśraya): one's own intelligence, strength, friends, learned rishis, wealth, ancestors, and demigods. Turning back to Krishna is the essence of the Govardhan-līlā and of the Krishna consciousness movement itself. Behind any chanting technique there must be this genuine change of attitude, and its one means is to 'drink the Bhāgavata' — relishing nectarean hearing about Krishna until He again occupies the heart's most significant place, making shelter in Him practical rather than theoretical (a devotee is a bhakta, not a believer in techniques). When the soul turns away it is gripped by Maya and fear and its conception of life is reversed (viparyaya/asmṛti) — becoming Krishna's competitor rather than His servant — and the remedy is to worship the Lord daily as master, deity, and source of life through unalloyed devotional service. The name itself is eternally pure (nitya-śuddha) and can never be contaminated — like the sun shining on impurity yet never made impure — as Gopāla Guru taught the young Caitanya Mahāprabhu, so it can and should be chanted everywhere and always. Because devotees need not remain neophytes, the speaker then teaches the deeper philosophy of japa from Dhyāna-candra Gosvāmī's paddhati: first, the mantra must be seated in the core of the mind by careful hearing, the sound passing through the aural channel into the heart; second, the mind must be fixed in the core of the mantra — its innermost essence, the Iṣṭa-devatā, who in this yuga-mantra is Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa. The symptom of real japa is equilibrium — a calm, balanced mind in which the vṛttis no longer move. The mantra opens with 'Hare,' a call to Rādhārāṇī as the Lord's energy whose response is 'what do you want?' and whose answer is 'please engage me in Your service'; theologically, since Bhakti Devi is an expansion of Rādhārāṇī, it is She who opens the door into the holy name. Practically, the speaker teaches training the wandering mind: gather all mental energy onto the very first 'Hare' of each round and keep returning to it; the mind 'is not chewing gum,' so bring it back deliberately, and attentive effort yields a perceptible inner 'miracle.' Attention is trained progressively — first present with the first Hare, then with each of the sixteen names one at a time — then chanting at one's own pace by determination rather than group momentum, praying to Lord Caitanya and the Pañca-tattva for the strength to concentrate. Single-pointed focus means resting on one name and forgetting all else, avoiding 'mental mush'; the sole prerequisite is the will to do it. Yet the speaker closes by insisting this victory over the mind is impossible by one's own strength — it comes only by the mercy of Krishna and the devotees. Forcing the mind ('boxing with the mind,' chanting with tension and aggression) backfires, just as 'don't think of pizza' fixes the mind on pizza and a brahmacārī's violent aversion fixes it on the object; the correct mood is gentle, surrendered dependency and helplessness before Krishna, not aggressive self-effort.

## Practical application

Treat inattentive japa as the one obstacle to overcome, and address it first as an attitude, not a technique. Begin with honest self-inventory using Bhurijana Prabhu's litmus test: when trouble comes — illness, accusation, misunderstanding, conflict — notice where you instinctively run for shelter. If it is your own intelligence, strength, friends, learned people, wealth, ancestors, or authorities (the seven anya-āśraya of Govardhan) rather than Krishna, that reveals where your heart actually rests, and it is exactly this tendency you reverse in chanting. Feed the change of attitude by 'drinking the Bhāgavata' daily — relishing hearing about Krishna until He again holds the most significant place in your heart, so that taking shelter in Him becomes real. Then apply the graded japa training: (1) hear each syllable very carefully so the sound passes through the ear into the heart and the mantra is seated in the core of the mind; (2) at the start of each round, collect all your mental energy and fix it on the very first 'Hare,' addressing Rādhārāṇī and praying to be engaged in service, and keep bringing the mind back to that first Hare whenever it wanders — actively, deliberately, since 'the mind is not chewing gum'; (3) progress from being present with the first Hare to being consciously present with each of the sixteen names, one at a time, giving each full attention; (4) then chant at your own pace, sustained by determination rather than group momentum; and aim always to bring the mind to Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa within the mantra, resting single-pointedly on one name and avoiding vague 'mental mush.' Frame the whole effort in dependency: before demanding chanting, pray to Lord Caitanya and the Pañca-tattva for the strength to concentrate; do not chant with a hard face, tension, or aggressive willpower ('boxing with the mind'), because forceful suppression backfires. Instead chant gently, in a mood of surrendered helplessness, begging the mercy of Krishna and the devotees — remembering that the name is eternally pure and can be chanted everywhere at all times, and that the only real prerequisite is simply the will to do it.

## Glossary

- **Harinām Cintāmaṇi** — The work by Haridāsa Ṭhākura on the science and offenses of chanting the holy name, which this seminar expounds.
- **pramāda** — Negligence or inattentiveness in chanting; here identified as the single root obstacle and the source of all other chanting offenses.
- **mada** — Madness; the speaker glosses pramāda through mada — the intensified madness of the conditioned soul turning away from Krishna.
- **japa** — Soft, personal recitation of the holy name, usually on beads.
- **kīrtan** — Congregational or vocal chanting/singing of the holy names.
- **mahā-mantra** — The great chant 'Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa, Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare / Hare Rāma Hare Rāma, Rāma Rāma Hare Hare' of sixteen names.
- **Maya** — The Lord's illusory material energy; the 'side' occupied by souls seeking happiness independent of Krishna.
- **Govardhan-līlā** — Krishna's pastime of lifting Govardhan Hill for seven days to protect Vraja from Indra's flood, teaching exclusive shelter in Him.
- **anya-āśraya** — Taking shelter in something other than Krishna; the seven false shelters — intelligence, strength, friends, sages, wealth, ancestors, demigods — corresponding to the seven days of Govardhan.
- **buddhi** — One's own intelligence — one of the false shelters.
- **bala** — One's own strength or power — one of the false shelters.
- **ṛṣi** — Wise or learned sages — one of the false shelters.
- **dhana** — Wealth — one of the false shelters.
- **pitṛ** — Ancestors — one of the false shelters.

- **bhakta** — A devotee — one who depends on devotion and relationship with Krishna rather than on mere techniques.
- **bhakti** — Devotional service; the only means of entering the chanting of the mahā-mantra.

- **viparyaya** — Reversal — the reversed conception of life whereby the soul takes itself to be Krishna's competitor rather than His servant.
- **asmṛti** — Forgetfulness (of one's true identity as Krishna's eternal servant), accompanying the reversed conception of life.
- **nitya-śuddha** — Eternally pure; the quality of the holy name, which like the sun can never be contaminated by impurity.
- **paddhati** — A manual of devotional procedure; here Dhyāna-candra Gosvāmī's paddhati on the stages of real japa.
- **Iṣṭa-devatā** — The worshipable Lord at the core of the mantra — in the yuga-mantra, Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa.
- **yuga-mantra** — The mantra prescribed for the present age (the mahā-mantra), whose presiding Lord is Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa.
- **vṛtti** — Mental agitation or fluctuation; in real japa the vṛttis cease and the mind attains equilibrium.
- **Hare** — The opening word of the mantra; a call to Rādhārāṇī as 'Hari's energy,' opening the mantra as a prayer to be engaged in service.
- **Pañca-tattva** — Caitanya Mahāprabhu with His four principal associates, prayed to for the strength to concentrate in chanting.
- **brahmacārī** — A celibate student; cited in the example of one who wrongly tries to conquer attraction by forceful aversion.
