# SNS Retreat Holy Name Retreat 10 Questions and Retreat 2022 11 16

## Theme

Delivered as the closing question-and-answer session of a Holy-Name retreat, this lecture by HH Sacīnandana Mahārāja maps the whole arc of how a devotee learns to chant the Holy Name with attention, taste, and a living relationship — and then how to keep what is gained once the retreat ends. It begins with the foundational disposition: when one cannot connect with the Name, the fault is placed on oneself, not on the Name, in the humble mood of Mahāprabhu's third Śikṣāṣṭaka verse, which naturally turns the chanter prayerful and dependent on Krishna's mercy. From here Mahārāja locates the work of chanting in the spiritual heart — distinct from the physical and emotional heart — which must be deliberately exercised through reading scripture, praying, and repeating the Ācāryas' prayers (such as Śaraṇāgati) that 'jumpstart' bhakti until genuine devotional feeling arises. He shows how the sequence of hearing and chanting (śravaṇam, kīrtanam) is enlivened only when active remembrance (smaraṇam) is brought through the heart, so that bhajan does not go dead and mechanical. The middle of the talk is intensely practical: because the soul is superior to the mind and can intervene to shift its own mood, a dull or tamasic state is not to be passively suffered through sixteen distracted rounds but actively changed — by walking, reading Śaraṇāgati, singing in a favourite tune, or call-and-response kīrtan. Yet techniques are secondary; the first realization a practitioner must reach is that chanting well genuinely matters, and instructions only penetrate a heart that already sincerely wants to reform. Proper chanting aligns body, mind, and heart toward Krishna, addressing the Name in the simple prayerful mood given by Gopāla Guru Gosvāmī and Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura: 'attract my heart to You; make me qualified for Your service.' Mahārāja then treats the obstacles directly — chanting as a powerful sadhana that can be marred by offenses (aparādhas), the chief of which during chanting itself is inattention (pramāda), the wandering mind from which all other offenses spring. He explains, via Viśvanātha Cakravartī's analogy of the master and the wayward servant, that offenses cause Krishna to withdraw the nourishing taste while still maintaining the offender, and that the Padma Purāṇa's remedy is simply to keep chanting — refusing to quit the relationship — until the dryness is removed. Throughout he stresses progressive sadhana (Bhaktivinoda's teaching that Krishna consciousness must remain an exciting reach toward the next level), the living two-way exchange in which the Name perceives and reciprocates with the chanter, and 'lucid living' rather than anxiety over dreams. The latter portion turns outward to service and kīrtana leadership: humble, regular service teaches the art of prayer and dependence on an all-powerful Master; preaching and seeing everything as Krishna's energy keeps consciousness fixed on Him; and a 'nice kīrtana' is defined by its purpose — the pleasure of Krishna — not its outward dressing, the leader deflecting attention from himself so the assembly can absorb themselves in the Name. The talk closes on retention: do not become a 'retreat junkie' dependent on one environment, but take notes on what truly struck you, isolate a single sustainable practice, and actually do it — because one insight internalized and carried into daily life awakens a dormant strength and becomes a permanent inner acquisition that nothing external can confiscate, while learning ultimately to 'fly unaided' for the moment of death.

## Practical application

For daily japa, begin from the humble premise of the third Śikṣāṣṭaka verse: when connection fails, do not blame the Name but turn the lack of connection into a prayer for mercy. Before or during rounds, deliberately exercise the spiritual heart — read about Krishna (e.g. the Nectar of Devotion) so you remember whose name you chant, and recite the Ācāryas' prayers such as Śaraṇāgati to 'jumpstart' devotional feeling until your own taste catches. Build the practice in sequence: hear and chant, then slowly remember (smaraṇam) what you heard, so chanting does not become mechanical; while chanting, direct consciousness to the heart, but lean on becoming genuinely prayerful rather than on technique alone. Treat your mood as something the soul can change, not endure: if you sit down dull or tamasic, get up and walk, read prayers, sing in a favourite tune, or do call-and-response kīrtan to shift the energy before grinding out distracted rounds dwelling on a shopping list, an offense, or a fear. Chant attentively by aligning body (sit upright, pronounce with the lips, listen with the ears), mind (focus on the sound), and heart (the prayerful mood 'attract my heart to You, make me qualified for Your service'). Guard attention as the central battle, since inattention (pramāda) is the chief offense from which others spring: prefer the early-morning sattva hours, arrange your lifestyle to use them, chant before an altar or in the temple, keep deity photos before you and fix your gaze on them or on the written mantra, and chant in the presence of Tulasī where possible. When intensive chanting brings no result, do not quit — turn it into inquiry, consult guidance (such as Prabhupāda's statements), pick one recommendation that appeals to you, and practice it deliberately for a set period. If taste is lost through offense, recognize that Krishna has only stepped back and that the condition will pass; keep chanting per the Padma Purāṇa to show you are not abandoning the relationship, and pray for taste itself as the maintenance of your spiritual life. Hold sixteen rounds as a floor, never a ceiling; remember Krishna through the day by taking deep morning darśana and fixing a saṃskāra of His form, by doing humble regular service that teaches dependence, and by carrying Kṛṣṇa conscious thoughts forward into 'lucid living.' Hear your own japa, not a recording of another's. And to retain anything gained from a retreat or intense period: take notes on the moments that struck you, choose just ONE practice you can actually sustain, and do it — a single internalized step becomes a permanent inner acquisition and awakens strength that was not there before, whereas trying to carry everything lets it all dissolve.

## Glossary

- **Hari-Nāma** — The Holy Name of the Lord; here, the chanting of Krishna's names as one's central spiritual practice.
- **japa** — Soft, individual repetition of the Holy Name, typically counted on beads; the daily personal chanting (e.g. sixteen rounds).
- **Mahā-mantra** — The 'great chant' — the sixteen-name Hare Krishna mantra repeated in japa and kīrtana.
- **Śikṣāṣṭaka** — The eight verses composed by Caitanya Mahāprabhu on the chanting of the Holy Name; the third verse teaches the humble mood of chanting.
- **Mahāprabhu** — Caitanya Mahāprabhu, regarded as the originator of the saṅkīrtana movement of chanting the Holy Name.
- **Ācāryas** — Authoritative spiritual teachers in the disciplic line whose prayers and example are followed; their composed prayers 'jumpstart' devotion.
- **bhakti** — Loving devotional service to Krishna; the inner devotional feeling that borrowed prayers are meant to awaken.
- **bhajan / bhajana** — One's devotional worship or practice; said to go 'dead' and mechanical without active remembrance.
- **sādhana** — Regulated devotional practice undertaken to make progress; to be performed progressively toward the next level.
- **sādhya** — The goal or perfection to be attained (e.g. constant, effortless chanting), as distinct from the practice (sādhana) that leads to it.
- **śravaṇam** — Hearing about Krishna — the first limb of devotional practice.
- **kīrtanam / kīrtana** — Chanting or congregational singing of the Holy Name; here also the led, call-and-response singing meant for Krishna's pleasure.
- **smaraṇam / smaraṇa** — Remembrance of Krishna; recalling what one has heard or read, which enlivens hearing and chanting and is one with constant chanting.
- **aparādha** — Offense against the Holy Name or devotees that obstructs the results of chanting and causes Krishna to withdraw taste.
- **pramāda** — Inattention or the wandering mind during chanting — named the chief offense, from which other offenses spring.
- **sattva-guṇa** — The mode of goodness, prominent in the early-morning hours and conducive to attentive chanting.
- **tamas / tamasic** — The mode of ignorance, producing dullness and reluctance; a state to be shifted (toward rajas) by interventions like walking.
- **rajas** — The mode of passion or activity; a more active state one may move into from tamas in order to re-enliven chanting.
- **saṃskāra** — A lasting impression fixed in consciousness — e.g. the form of the deities taken in through morning darśana.
- **darśana** — The act of seeing and being seen by the deity; deep morning darśana fixes a remembered impression for the day.
- **niṣṭhā** — Steadiness or firm fixity in devotional practice; the urge to display such advanced standing can become self-deception.
- **nirjana-bhajana** — Solitary worship in seclusion; imitating it (as in imitating Haridāsa Ṭhākura) can be a deceptive trick of the mind in this age.
- **kaitava** — Cheating or deception; here, the mind's trick of wanting to display advanced renunciation or constant chanting.
- **Śaraṇāgati** — A collection of surrender prayers by Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura; reciting them disciplines the mind and rekindles devotional feeling.
- **Tulasī (Devī)** — The sacred plant worshipped as a great devotee; chanting in her presence, after the example of Haridāsa Ṭhākura and the ācāryas, is especially powerful.
- **Vaiṣṇavas** — Devotees of Krishna; criticizing them is an offense that tends to occur outside the act of chanting.
- **Gaura-Nitāi** — Caitanya Mahāprabhu and Nityānanda Prabhu; their images are kept before the chanter as aids to attention.
