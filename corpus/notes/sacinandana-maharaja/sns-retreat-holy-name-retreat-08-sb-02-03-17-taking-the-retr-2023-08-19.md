# SNS Retreat Holy Name Retreat 08 SB 02 03 17 Taking the Retreat Home 2023 08 19 Slovenia

## Theme

Delivered as the closing session of a Holy-Name retreat, this lecture answers the parting question: what do we make of all this, and how is it useful for our lives? Sacīnandana Mahārāja's central thesis is to 'take the retreat home' — to translate the retreat experience into daily living rather than leaving its effect behind. He grounds this in the discovery that because the retreat focused on the holy name, Krishna reciprocated with everyone: sincere effort to turn to Krishna draws a response from above that widens our small world. Improving one's chanting, he insists, is the very same act as improving one's relationship with Krishna; chanting is not primarily a meditation technique but a devotional practice of connecting with, turning to, and serving Krishna, and wherever it is done as service, reciprocation follows. Through it we move from the 'small mind' to the 'big mind,' from a 'human doing' into a 'human being.' He then situates this in the urgency of time: with every sunrise and sunset the duration of life is irretrievably taken away, and a fraction wasted cannot be bought back with any amount of gold — the sole exception being time spent chanting and glorifying Krishna (uttama-śloka-vārtā), which does not diminish life but extends it into eternity. The human form exists solely so one may realize his spiritual identity and lost relationship with the Lord, and the very meaning of sādhana lies in 'sat,' the eternal; of all sādhanas, chanting the holy name is for the Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas the most efficient and beneficial. The heart of the talk is realistic: a 'peak' experience cannot be sustained because we live in the valley, not on the summit, so what we carry home is not the euphoria but a lesson — the reliable, transferable insight that focus on the Holy Name brings wonderful reciprocation. Real transformation does not happen by wish alone; like the caterpillar that becomes a butterfly only when an internal program is activated, one must deliberately install an inner program of change. Mahārāja lays out this 'Dynamics of Change' as staged work: honest self-assessment of one's actual japa habits, projecting their five-year trajectory to generate motivation, building faith before mechanics, preparing one's physical space and oneself holistically (physical, mental, emotional, spiritual — illustrated by acclimatizing before Mt. Kailash), making a realistic plan, acting, then re-evaluating after about a week. This process, he says, is as certain as gravity: work it sincerely and it is impossible to remain the same. What usually blocks people is not philosophical doubt but lack of faith in themselves — which is a lack of faith in the possibility of change — and that faith must be cultivated through scripture and the association of inspiring devotees. He offers a portable daily formula — Intention, Sincerity, and Attention (begin with saṅkalpa, chant from the genuinely spiritual part of oneself, stay attentive) — and the benediction 'Kalyāṇam astu.' For the times when no sādhana works and one cannot even set the saṅkalpa, he gives a fail-safe remedy: do not hide and isolate (which only feeds a downward spiral of material information and diminishing taste), but go back into the association of devotees and serve them with concrete, physical work — even simple cleaning — because lack of taste is a 'mind lock' that introspection cannot open; taste for vāsudeva-kathā is reborn through service, and fellow devotees 'know where the key is.' Lack of taste is not a personal failing but a covering by the modes of nature; he even notes an Āyurvedic aid (depression rooted in tamas and rajas, lifted by serving cows, who abide in sattva — hence gośālā service). Finally, drawing on Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura, he honors two legitimate, God-given devotional temperaments — the structured (kaniṣṭha-adhikārī) and the spontaneous/inspired (rāgānuga) — held together by a common non-negotiable floor of the four regulative principles and sixteen rounds daily, urging the community to bring both natures together.

## Practical application

On returning home from any elevated experience, do not try to recreate the peak; instead carry one clear insight down into ordinary life — above all the lived discovery that focusing on the Holy Name brings reciprocation. Treat improving your chanting as identical to improving your relationship with Krishna, and chant as service rather than as a technique. Begin the reform deliberately. First, sit down and conduct a brutally honest, private audit of your real japa day: when you rise, how many rounds, whether you distribute them, and whether you chant with absorption or superficially, distractedly, or lazily — there is no one to impress, so do not flatter yourself. Then project where that practice will be in five years if nothing changes; let the unappealing trajectory supply your motivation. Work on faith before mechanics — resolving to chant more rounds will not last unless it rests on conviction that chanting is good for you, so identify your inner 'blocks' and the discouragement of past failures and build faith through suitable scripture and the association of inspired devotees. Prepare the ground: put your physical space in order (a clean altar, a writing table, a designated place to sit) to clear out the old and make room for the new; and prepare yourself holistically — drop contrary habits like late-night binge-watching, read about Krishna beforehand to draw the consciousness toward Him, eat lighter, and get some exercise so the body can sit and concentrate, just as one acclimatizes before high altitude. Make a realistic plan, put it into action, and re-evaluate after about a week, adjusting anything unrealistic. For the chanting itself, carry the simple portable formula: Intention — open with a conscious saṅkalpa; Sincerity — chant from the genuinely spiritual part of yourself, the part that first responded to the call to become a devotee; and Attention — stay as attentive as you can throughout. Hold the baseline without compromise: the four regulative principles and a minimum of sixteen rounds daily — but beyond that floor, honor your own God-given temperament, whether you thrive on structure and schedules or move by inspiration, and respect that others differ. And keep the emergency remedy ready for the days nothing works and you cannot even set the saṅkalpa or find the part of you that wants to chant: resist the instinct to hide, isolate, and fill the mind with the internet, which only deepens the spiral. Instead go back into the association of devotees and serve them with concrete, physical work — even cleaning a room or doing a day of gośālā service — because a 'mind lock' opens through action, not introspection, and taste for hearing about Krishna is reborn there. Do not let shame or fear keep you away; the devotees are kind, they know where the key is, and turning toward them is precisely the way out.

## Glossary

- **Hari-Nāma / holy name** — The name of Krishna (Hari); chanting it is the central devotional practice of the retreat — not a meditation technique but an act of connecting with, turning to, and serving Krishna.
- **japa** — The personal, repeated chanting of the holy name, typically counted in rounds; the daily practice the lecture seeks to reform.
- **sādhana** — Regulated spiritual practice; its meaning is rooted in 'sat' (the eternal), so it prepares one for what cannot be taken away.
- **sat** — The eternal, the real; the syllable at the root of 'sādhana,' indicating that genuine practice aims at the everlasting.
- **uttama-śloka-vārtā** — Talk and glorification of Krishna, the one of all-excellent renown; time spent in it does not diminish life but extends it into eternity.
- **Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas** — The tradition of Krishna devotion stemming from Bengal/Gauḍa in which chanting the holy name is held to be the most efficient and beneficial of all sādhanas.
- **saṅkalpa** — A conscious resolve or vow made at the start of practice; the 'Intention' step of the daily formula.
- **vāsudeva-kathā** — Hearing and discussion about Krishna (Vāsudeva); the taste for it is born through serving devotees, not by forcing the mind.
- **modes of nature** — The three guṇas — sattva (goodness), rajas (passion), and tamas (ignorance) — whose covering, rather than personal failing, accounts for a loss of taste.
- **sattva** — The mode of goodness; the cow abides in it, so serving cows can lift a covered, depressed consciousness.
- **rajas** — The mode of passion; together with tamas, a root of the depressed, covered mind.
- **tamas** — The mode of ignorance/darkness; with rajas, underlies depression in the Āyurvedic diagnosis.
- **Āyurveda** — The traditional Indic system of medicine; offers a remedy 'one step below sādhana' for the covered, depressed mind, such as serving cows.
- **gośālā** — A cow shelter; spending a day in its service (cleaning, feeding) is recommended to raise the consciousness toward sattva.
- **Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura** — Vaiṣṇava teacher whose writings explain the two legitimate devotional temperaments — the structured and the spontaneous — as rooted in different God-given natures.
- **kaniṣṭha-adhikārī** — A term Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura applies loosely here not as a rank of advancement but as a temperament: the 'structured' devotee, drawn to clear rules, schedules, and definitions.
- **rāgānuga** — Likewise used loosely as a temperament: the more 'spontaneous,' inspired devotee who acts by inspiration rather than by a catalog of rules.
- **four regulative principles** — The non-negotiable baseline of conduct that cannot be broken if one wants freedom from material influence.
- **sixteen rounds** — The minimum daily quota of japa forming, with the four regulative principles, the common floor for every devotee regardless of temperament.
- **Kalyāṇam astu** — 'May there be auspiciousness for you' — the parting benediction in the mood of the great devotees of India.
