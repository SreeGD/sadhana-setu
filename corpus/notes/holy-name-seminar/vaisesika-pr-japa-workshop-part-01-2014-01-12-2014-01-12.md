# Vaisesika Pr Japa Workshop Part 01 2014 01 12

## Theme

This opening session of Vaiśeṣika Dāsa's japa workshop builds a complete case for japa as genuine meditation and then trains the participants in how to do it. It begins with the Bhagavad-gītā's teaching that controlling the mind is central to all bhakti scriptures: the mind, restless as the wind, is tamed by suitable practice and detachment, where detachment means seeing everything as owned and controlled by Īśvara rather than abruptly renouncing possessions. Meditation also demands a clean, truthful supporting lifestyle, because what we eat, hear, and associate with shapes the mind. The speaker then maps the inner anatomy: the soul inhabits the body like a machine or a city of nine gates, watches the mind's stream like a movie, and is carried by the mind at death to its next destination — hence Krishna's instruction to think of Him. Since the mind cannot rest on voidness and demands engagement, the mahā-mantra — etymologically 'that which delivers the mind' — is the engagement that finally satisfies it, and Patañjali's own shortcut, īśvara-praṇidhāna (submission to the Supersoul), points the same way. Japa is thus classical one-pointed meditation that connects one directly to Īśvara, because the mantra is a spiritual formula, the supreme combination of syllables, non-different from the Lord Himself. The session teachings follow: suspend disbelief, meditate on one's utter dependence, chant audibly to oneself like a child calling its mother, and gently return the wandering mind to the sound without anxiety or count-watching. A long reading from a Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam purport (SB 2.2.35) grounds the practice philosophically — distinguishing seer from seen, establishing matter, spirit, and super-spirit, and showing intelligence itself as the Paramātmā's direction — while Nārada's history shows that realization comes by grace, not mechanical effort. The seminar closes with practicals: the three P's (posture, prayer, pronunciation, with prayer foremost), fixed daily rounds, remedies for dull or doubting days and for sleepiness, Viśvanātha Cakravartī's five obstacles that chanting itself cleanses, and the assurance that for one who goes on chanting, success is certain: the chanting that begins as medicine becomes the very food that sustains the soul.

## Practical application

Set up a fixed daily japa practice, even starting with five minutes: choose a regular time (ideally early morning, having slept on time and eaten lightly the night before), and create a sacred, distraction-free space with a seat that keeps the hips above the knees and the spine straight. Before chanting, take a deep breath and relax on the exhale, suspend disbelief, and center yourself on the truths of the session — you are not the body, you depend on the Supersoul for your very breath, heartbeat, and intelligence, and there is nothing to be had in the fourteen worlds except this mantra. Then chant just loud enough for your own ears, concentrating on the sound you yourself make, in the mood of a helpless child calling its mother: 'O my Lord, O energy of the Lord, please engage me in Your service.' The core method is simple: when you notice the mind has wandered — and it will, every half-second — don't become anxious or scold yourself; note it with detached amusement and, like a patient parent with a restless child, bring the attention back to the mantra, taking shelter of your feeling of dependence. Apply the three P's: sit perfectly still with a straight back (posture), chant prayerfully rather than mechanically, aware that the name is Krishna Himself (prayer — the most important), and articulate every syllable, occasionally recording and reviewing your own japa to catch dropped syllables (pronunciation). Keep a fixed daily quota of rounds (108 mantras per round on beads) for steadiness, but in absorption-oriented sessions set aside count-anxiety and lose yourself in the sound. On days of low enthusiasm, chant with enthusiastic devotees, experiment with a better place to chant, or study verses glorifying the name so fresh appreciation draws the mind in; if the rounds seem to produce no effect, chant without stoppage. Support the practice with daily Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam reading, pray each session for even a mustard seed of grace, never leave the beads feeling you got nothing (something is always given, often recognized later), and go on chanting with great courage — success is certain.

## Glossary

- **japa** — Individual meditative chanting of a mantra, classically counted on beads; fixing the mind one-pointedly on the sound of the holy name.
- **Hari-Nāma** — The holy name of the Lord (Hari); the practice of chanting God's names.
- **mahā-mantra** — The 'great mantra' — Hare Krishna Hare Krishna Krishna Krishna Hare Hare / Hare Rama Hare Rama Rama Rama Hare Hare; 16 words and 32 syllables. As the omnibus mantra, everything is contained within it.
- **mantra** — From man (mind) and tra (to free or deliver): a spiritual formula of syllables that frees the mind by giving it an engagement that finally satisfies it.
- **Hare** — Address to the Lord's energy, His internal potency.
- **Krishna** — The all-attractive Supreme Person, source of everything.
- **Rama** — The highest spiritual happiness, which everyone is actually seeking.
- **Īśvara** — The Supreme Controller and owner of everything; the Lord who supplies intelligence and maintains all beings.
- **Paramātmā (Supersoul)** — The Lord's plenary representation seated in the heart — the 'second bird' on the tree of the body — who witnesses, guides, and supplies intelligence to the individual soul.
- **bhakti** — Devotional service to the Supreme Lord; the path whose scriptures center on redirecting the mind and attention to Him.
- **aṣṭāṅga-yoga** — The classical eight-limbed yoga system of Patañjali, beginning with ethical purification and culminating in one-pointed meditation.
- **īśvara-praṇidhāna** — Offering oneself in supplication and obeisance to Īśvara, the Supersoul; given by Patañjali in the Yoga-sūtra as the shortcut to the entire yoga process.
- **man-manā bhava mad-bhakto** — Krishna's instruction in the Gita: 'Always think of Me and become My devotee' — fix the mind and consciousness on Him.
- **yantra** — Machine; the Gita's metaphor for the material body the soul rides in, also compared to a city of nine gates.
- **cañcala** — Restless, flickering — the mind's innate nature, as Arjuna admitted; the very reason a process of practice exists.
- **param akṣarākṛte** — Rūpa Gosvāmī's description of the prescribed mantra as the supreme combination of syllables.
- **amūrtikam** — 'Formless' — glossed by Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura to mean the Lord has no material form; His spiritual form is realized easily by one who concentrates on the mantra's sound.
- **āsana** — Yogic sitting posture; its real purpose is to keep the body steady and restless-free for long meditation.
- **cintāmaṇi** — Touchstone; the scriptures glorify the holy name as a wish-fulfilling gem that grants all desires.
- **jñāna** — Knowledge; arises automatically from direct contact with the holy name.
- **vairāgya** — Detachment; seeing everything in proper perspective as Krishna's property — also arises automatically from chanting.
- **laya** — Sleep — the first of Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura's five persistent obstacles to chanting.
- **vikṣepa** — Distraction — the second of the five obstacles to chanting.
- **apratipatti** — Indifference to the chanting even when sleep and distraction are absent — the third obstacle.
- **kaṣāya** — Innate lower tendencies such as anger, lust, and greed surfacing during chanting — the fourth obstacle.
- **rasāsvāda** — The pull of material sense pleasure while chanting — the fifth obstacle; all five are gradually cleansed by continuing to chant.
- **round** — One circuit of 108 mantras chanted on japa beads; devotees commit to a fixed number of rounds daily, tracked with counter beads.
- **three modes of material nature** — The conditioning influences (goodness, passion, ignorance) that shape each person's belief system, making the same situation appear happy to one and lamentable to another.
- **Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam** — The bhakti scripture whose Second Canto the speaker reads from; every devotee is urged to own a set and study it daily to nourish japa.
- **Harināma Cintāmaṇi** — Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's treatise on the holy name, cited for the advice to do whatever is necessary to get into a situation where one can chant.
- **Vāsudeva** — The Supreme Personality of Godhead, Krishna, at whose lotus feet the intelligent person surrenders after many births.
