# Vaisesika Pr Harinama Harinama in Downtown Mountain View 2017 07 08

## Theme

This is a live recording of the first inaugural public harinām held at the steps of Mountain View City Hall (July 8, 2017), led by Vaiśeṣika Dāsa. The event opens exactly like a temple program — the full sequence of Gauḍīya praṇāma-mantras honouring the guru-paramparā, the Vaiṣṇavas, Pañca-tattva, Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa and Lord Nṛsiṁha — deliberately situating street chanting within the disciplic succession rather than as informal performance. The invocation lays the śāstric foundation: the Lord descends age after age to re-establish dharma (BG 4.7–8), and hearing and chanting about Kṛṣṇa cleanses the heart, cuts the knot of doubts and karma, and fixes one in devotional service (SB 1.2.17–21); congregational chanting (saṅkīrtana) is announced as the yuga-dharma, the prescribed practice for this age, offered inclusively to the whole city. Between kirtans the speaker develops the theology of sacred sound: kirtan is an ancient yoga of hearing rooted in the śruti tradition — we become what we hear — and whereas sages once withdrew to forests to hear only transcendental mantra, in Kali-yuga devotees invert the method and bring the transcendental sound into the cities. The mahā-mantra, given in the Kali-Santaraṇa Upaniṣad, frees one from all anxiety; 'mantra' itself means that which delivers the mind; its 16 words and 32 syllables are verifiable by personal experiment and can be chanted silently, softly, or sung aloud. The names Hare, Kṛṣṇa and Rāma are transcendental persons who enter through the ears and transform the heart. The event consciously re-enacts the movement's founding: the site was chosen for a tree nearly identical to the one under which Śrīla Prabhupāda first chanted in New York — a kalpa-vṛkṣa — and, following Caitanya Mahāprabhu's own pattern of kirtan moving from Śrīvāsa's courtyard into the streets, the devotees frame this as continuing the 'Hare Krishna explosion' from a hub (Silicon Valley) meant to reach the whole world. Playing on Google's nearby research into sound and health, the local temple is presented as a 'spiritual hospital' and holy-name research laboratory, and passers-by are bridged into ongoing association through an invitation to the Sunday feast. The kirtan itself weaves in the Nṛsiṁha praṇāma and Jayadeva Gosvāmī's Daśāvatāra hymn, invoking the Lord's protection within saṅkīrtana. The lecture culminates with Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja's image of Caitanya and Nityānanda as the sun and moon rising over Gauḍa to illuminate the heart itself — harinām as Gaura-Nitāi's mercy drawing people out of cars, restaurants and apartments without prior qualification — and, as the full moon rises, the speaker declares the street kirtan a Guru Pūrṇimā yajña, a formal sacrifice offered under the shelter of the local presiding Deities.

## Practical application

Treat chanting itself as the meditation: the practice needs no special setting and can be done at home, at work, or in a public square, so a busy devotee can integrate it into any schedule — even a little bhakti-yoga added to a full working life gives perspective, detachment, and happiness awakened from within rather than from externals. Use the three modes the lecture prescribes as a daily framework: chant silently in the mind, chant just loud enough to hear yourself (japa), or sing aloud with music (kirtan). Approach the mahā-mantra as a personal experiment — chant its 16 words and 32 syllables attentively and observe its transformative effect for yourself, remembering that the names are transcendental persons entering through the ears, so chant with pure intention and hear attentively, since we become what we hear and the sound we expose ourselves to shapes our consciousness. Begin chanting sessions the way the harinām began: with maṅgalācaraṇa, invoking guru, Śrīla Prabhupāda, the paramparā and the Vaiṣṇavas, so that japa and kirtan are performed under shelter rather than as informal exercise, and include prayers for protection such as the Nṛsiṁha praṇāma. Participate in public harināma as the yuga-dharma — carrying transcendental sound into the city is the Kali-yuga inversion of the sages' forest retreat, and it purifies both the chanter and everyone who hears without their needing prior qualification. Finally, treat outreach as a bridge: pair the street encounter with a concrete welcoming next step, inviting people into ongoing association at the temple (e.g., the Sunday feast with spiritual discussion, singing, dancing, and prasādam).

## Glossary

- **harinām / harināma** — Public congregational chanting of the holy names of the Lord, typically processing through streets and public spaces.
- **saṅkīrtana** — Congregational chanting of Kṛṣṇa's divine names; presented in the lecture as the yuga-dharma for this age.
- **yuga-dharma** — The prescribed religious practice for a particular cosmic age; in Kali-yuga it is the congregational chanting of the holy name.
- **Kali-yuga** — The present degraded age in the Vedic cycle of ages, in which chanting the holy name is the recommended means of deliverance.
- **mahā-mantra** — The 'great mantra' — Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare / Hare Rāma Hare Rāma Rāma Rāma Hare Hare — 16 words and 32 syllables, given in the Kali-Santaraṇa Upaniṣad.
- **mantra** — Sacred sound formula; explained in the lecture as 'that which delivers the mind.'
- **maṅgalācaraṇa** — Auspicious invocation recited before a devotional program — the sequence of praṇāma-mantras honouring guru, the paramparā, the Vaiṣṇavas, Pañca-tattva, Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa and Lord Nṛsiṁha.
- **praṇāma-mantra** — A verse of obeisance offered to a guru or deity.
- **guru-paramparā** — The disciplic succession of spiritual masters through which teachings are transmitted.
- **śāstra / śāstric** — Revealed Vedic scripture; 'śāstric' means grounded in such scripture (e.g., Bhagavad-gītā, Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam).
- **śruti** — 'That which is heard' — the tradition of transmitting Vedic knowledge aurally from teacher to student across generations.
- **japa** — Private, meditative repetition of the mantra, typically soft enough that one hears oneself.
- **kirtan / kīrtana** — Chanting or singing the Lord's names and glories aloud, often with musical accompaniment.
- **bhakti-yoga** — The yoga of loving devotional service to Kṛṣṇa, of which chanting is a principal practice.
- **Kali-Santaraṇa Upaniṣad** — The Upaniṣad that gives the Hare Kṛṣṇa mahā-mantra as the means of crossing over the age of Kali, freeing the chanter from all anxieties.
- **Pañca-tattva** — The five features of the Absolute headed by Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu, honoured in the opening invocation.
- **Gaura-Nitāi** — Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu and Lord Nityānanda, likened to the sun and moon rising together over Gauḍa to illuminate the heart.
- **Nṛsiṁha praṇāma** — Prayers to Lord Narasiṁha, the half-man half-lion avatāra and protector of devotees, chanted for protection within the kirtan.
- **Daśāvatāra hymn** — Jayadeva Gosvāmī's song glorifying the ten avatāras of the Lord, including Narasiṁha.
- **avatāra** — A descent or incarnation of the Lord into the material world.
- **kalpa-vṛkṣa** — A spiritual 'desire tree' that fulfils all wishes; applied to the tree at the harinām site, likened to the New York tree under which Śrīla Prabhupāda first chanted publicly.
- **Guru Pūrṇimā** — The full-moon day honouring the spiritual master; the speaker declares the street kirtan a yajña offered on this occasion.
- **yajña** — A formal Vedic sacrifice; the public chanting is framed as such an offering rather than a casual performance.
- **Vrindavan (Vṛndāvana)** — The sacred village where Kṛṣṇa appeared, so saturated with remembrance of Kṛṣṇa and Rādhārāṇī that its residents greet one another with 'Radhe Radhe.'
- **prasādam** — Sanctified vegetarian food offered to the Lord and distributed, as at the Sunday feast.
