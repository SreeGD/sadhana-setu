# Vaisesika Pr Japa Workshop 2014 07 05 ISV

## Theme

This 2014 japa workshop by Vaiśeṣika Dāsa builds, step by step, a complete philosophy and practice of chanting the holy name. It opens with sincere inquiry — questions like 'why must I die?' draw answers from without and within, and because the Absolute Truth is unlimited, spiritual life makes one an eternal student. The Bhagavad-gītā (for the speaker, through Śrīla Prabhupāda's edition) supplies reasonable answers that satisfy both intellect and heart, and defines yoga from the root 'yuj': the living being lacks nothing except connection with its divine source — like a hand severed from the body — yet the soul is never actually disconnected; suffering comes only from the illusion of separation, so yoga simply means turning attention back to the ever-present source. On the principle that 'where attention goes, energy flows' (which advertising exploits in reverse), bhakti-yoga places attention on Krishna, the all-attractive. Its sublime meditation is the holy name because, on the absolute platform, the name and the named are identical (abhinnatvāt): the name is of the same spiritual energy as the soul itself — a living touchstone (cintāmaṇi, caitanya, rasa, vigraha), complete (pūrṇa), pure (śuddha), eternally liberated (nitya-mukta), and, per Rūpa Gosvāmī's poetic exception, even more merciful than the named because it is always at hand — water in the palm of a thirsty person, versus the cloud, the flash flood of an avatāra, or the deep well of classical yoga. Unlike a candle flame or the breath, the name is a person's intimate name and reciprocates, sustaining the practitioner with tangible progress. The workshop then frames japa within Krishna's answer to Arjuna's protest that the mind is cañcala, pramāthi, balavat, and dṛḍha: the mind is curbed by abhyāsa (daily, repeated, mindful practice that becomes an unbreakable rope of habit) and vairāgya (ocean-like indifference to the mind's provocations, like ignoring an unruly child). Practically this unfolds as the limbs of attentive japa: presence (distinguishing one's eternal 'life' from the mind-made 'life situation,' anchoring in breath and the question 'Am I okay right now?'), pronunciation (yoga begins with the tongue — chant slow and right, record and audit your own japa), posture (erect spine, sitting still to expose the mind's restlessness), and prayer (chanting as a spiritual cry of the child for the mother, addressing Harā and Hari, in Gajendra's mood of helplessness rather than the false ego's 'I'm okay already'). Supporting disciplines follow: begin with the Pañca-tattva mantra, protect an early undistracted chanting time, keep numerical strength (a fixed daily vow of rounds regardless of taste), chant in Lord Caitanya's humble state of mind, take advantage of association with absorbed chanters, experiment honestly with conditions as Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura recommends, and renew one's sankalpa daily. The closing session grounds everything in sound: consciousness is shaped by the sound one associates with, the mahā-mantra descends from the spiritual realm, and its proof is direct, progressive experience — like a hungry person feeling nourishment with every morsel, though the taste may arrive afterwards 'as a wave.' Chanting is both the means (sādhana) and the goal (sādhya), the especially effective practice for Kali-yuga, and the workshop ends experientially — a kīrtana demonstrating the congregational counterpart to japa, followed by guru-pūjā and prasādam, the sanctified food carrying the same spiritual vibration as the mantra.

## Practical application

Treat daily japa as the central point of the day and arrange everything else around it: carve out a protected time early in the morning when there is nothing else to do. Begin each session by centering yourself — settle into normal breathing (measured 4-4-4-4 breathing helps), notice the consciousness pervading the body, and recognize that you can place your attention wherever you wish; then chant the Pañca-tattva mantra to clear obstacles, meditating on the mercy each personality has shown you so that gratitude opens a prayerful mood. Fix a sankalpa — your determination for why you are chanting — and if no prayer arises naturally, keep a prayer of a great personality (such as the Īśopaniṣad prayer) beside you, returning to it whenever the mind wanders. While chanting, apply the limbs of practice: sit comfortably and still with hips above knees and spine slightly erect (stopping the habit of rocking exposes and curbs mental restlessness); pronounce each name slowly and distinctly with tongue, muscles, and vocal cords fully engaged, creating a private 'circle of sound' from lips to ears; and chant as a genuine call — the cry of a child for its mother — not mechanical recitation, listening to the sincerity of your own chanting as Prabhupāda advised. Handle the restless mind with abhyāsa and vairāgya: do the same practice mindfully every day, and be deliberately indifferent to the mind's promptings — neither chase distractions nor fight them, but gently return awareness to the mantra, staying unmoved like the ocean. When anxiety arises, ask 'Am I okay right now?' and distinguish your eternal life from your temporary life situation. Maintain numerical strength — chant your fixed number of rounds every day whether taste comes or not, remembering beads are not even required (fingers suffice) — and do not judge the japa by taste during the rounds, since the experience often comes afterwards as a wave. Audit yourself honestly: record your japa and play it back, never assume you are chanting perfectly, and periodically start over as a beginner. Avoid all 'absent-presence japa' (chanting while driving, computing, or ruminating); rearrange life daily so conscious chanting is possible. Cultivate humility as the mood of the whole practice — feel yourself an insignificant servant in a borrowed body, offer respect to others expecting none in return, and approach the name as a beggar for mercy rather than someone extracting a result. Finally, take strategic advantage of association: chant near serious, absorbed chanters, learn from other practitioners' experience, and remember that your own attentive chanting silently teaches and inspires everyone who sees it.

## Glossary

- **japa** — Private, personal meditation through soft repetition of the mantra, uttered just loudly enough for oneself to hear, creating a 'circle of sound' from lips to ears; distinguished from congregational kīrtana.
- **kīrtana** — Loud, melodic congregational chanting of the mahā-mantra with call and response, as opposed to quiet individual japa.
- **mahā-mantra** — The 'great mantra' of 16 words and 32 syllables (Hare Krishna, Hare Rama), containing the primary names of God; its meaning is 'O my Lord, O energy of the Lord, please engage me in Your service.'
- **mantra** — From 'man' (mind) and 'tra' (from trāyate, 'to deliver'): a sound vibration that delivers the mind from the world's lower vibrations by giving it a higher vibration to concentrate on.
- **yoga** — From the root 'yuj,' to connect: the process of restoring the living being's connection with its original divine source — the only thing it lacks.
- **bhakti-yoga** — The yoga of devotion, whose meditation deliberately places attention on Krishna to evoke the living entity's original attraction to the divine.
- **Krishna (Kṛṣṇa)** — Name of God meaning 'all-attractive'; the source of every opulence (beauty, power, knowledge, renunciation) sought in worldly relationships.
- **bhinnā** — 'Separated': describes Krishna's eight material energies — earth, water, fire, air, ether, mind, intelligence, and ego — as distinct from the higher spiritual energy, the soul.
- **abhinnatvāt** — 'Because of non-difference': the principle that the holy name is absolutely identical to the person named, so keeping attention on the name is direct contact with the Supreme.
- **cintāmaṇi** — Touchstone; the holy name is likened to a touchstone that transforms whatever it touches.
- **caitanya** — Fully alive, conscious — a quality of the holy name as a living spiritual entity, not dead sound.
- **rasa** — Taste, relationship; the holy name is full of rasa — flavor and personal relationship.
- **vigraha** — Form; the holy name contains the complete form of the Supreme.
- **pūrṇa** — Complete, lacking nothing — unlike the incomplete objects the world offers our attention.
- **śuddha** — Completely pure, containing nothing harmful — like food from one's own mother.
- **nitya-mukta** — Eternally liberated; the holy name is not bound by any law of the material world or its pattern of gain and loss.
- **avatāra** — A descent of God into the world; meeting one is compared to catching a flash flood — right place, right time — versus the name, always at hand.
- **cañcala** — Restless, flickering — Arjuna's first description of the mind, darting from one thing to the next.
- **pramāthi** — Mad, agitating — the mind suggests crazy things unprompted.
- **balavat** — Very strong — a quality of the mind that makes it hard to subdue.
- **dṛḍha** — Obstinate, stubborn — the fourth of Arjuna's descriptions of the uncontrollable mind.
- **abhyāsa** — Repeated, disciplined practice; per Monier-Williams, permanent exercise and the inculcation of a truth by repetition — mindfully doing the same thing every day; in yoga, the mind's effort to remain in its pure, unmodified condition.
- **vairāgya** — Detachment; practiced as deliberate indifference to the mind's provocations — like ignoring an unruly child until it quiets — remaining unmoved like the ocean as desires flow in.
- **sattva** — Purity, the mind's unmodified condition that abhyāsa strives to maintain.
- **dharma** — The eternal occupation of every living entity — service — connection to which produces happiness.
- **sat-cit-ānanda-vigraha** — Form of eternity, consciousness, and bliss; used to describe one's real 'life' (consciousness itself) as distinct from the mind-made 'life situation.'
- **bhāgavata-pradhāna** — The most exalted devotee — one who has captured the Lord within the heart through chanting with genuine love, whom Krishna can never give up.
- **kīrtanīyaḥ sadā hariḥ** — 'The Lord should be chanted always' — the fruit of Lord Caitanya's formula of humility: chanting constantly without impediment.
- **Harā** — Krishna's internal energy, addressed as 'Hare' in the mahā-mantra; she protects the conditioned soul and helps one achieve the grace of the spiritual father, Hari.
- **Hari** — Name of the Lord as the spiritual father whose grace the chanter seeks through Harā.
- **Pañca-tattva mantra** — The mantra 'jaya śrī-kṛṣṇa-caitanya prabhu nityānanda śrī-advaita gadādhara śrīvāsādi-gaura-bhakta-vṛnda,' chanted before japa to clear obstacles.
- **sankalpa** — The fixing of self-conception and determination — why one is chanting — set at the start of japa and renewed daily against the mind's erosion.
- **sādhana** — The practice, the means of spiritual realization; chanting is the sādhana of this path.
- **sādhya** — The goal; chanting is unique in being both means and goal — the same chanting continues in perfection.
- **Kali-yuga** — The present age, fraught with obstacles and distractions, for which chanting the holy name is the especially effective spiritual practice.
- **Hari-nāma-cintāmaṇi** — Work by Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura recommending honest experimentation with chanting conditions (solitary room, cloth over the head, beside an advanced chanter).
- **guru-pūjā** — Worship ('pūjā') of the teacher ('guru'); in ISKCON, the traditional ceremony honoring founder A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda with Bengali prayers and flower offerings.
- **simhāsana** — Seat of honor on which the guru's image is placed during guru-pūjā.
- **prasādam** — Food cooked with love as a conscious offering to Krishna and thereby transformed, carrying the same spiritual vibration as the mantra; eating it imparts spiritual acumen.
- **mṛdaṅga** — Drum used in devotional music; cited as the model for practice — you cannot practice fast and imperfectly; practice slow and right until you can go fast and right.
- **Gajendra** — The elephant of scripture who, seized by an alligator at the height of prosperity, called out for help — the model of genuine prayer arising from life's instability.
- **Naimiṣāraṇya** — The forest where assembled sages deliberated on the world's problems — evoked by devotees sitting together in group sādhana, controlling their minds in unison.
- **Rūpa Gosvāmī** — Devotional saint of 500 years ago who taught that, though name and named are non-different, the name is poetically more merciful — because it descends and is always at hand while the person may be out of reach.
- **Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura** — Ācārya who sang that there is nothing to be had in the fourteen worlds except the name, and who taught approaching the holy name as a beggar, since mercy can only be received, not demanded.
